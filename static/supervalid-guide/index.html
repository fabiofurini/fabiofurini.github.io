Supervalid inequalities: which roads must we hit?

Follow the shortest route, close a road, and discover why some feasible interdictions can be safely discarded during incumbent improvement.

The basic question

Suppose the best interdiction we currently know achieves a shortest-path distance of 5 from root 2 to destination 3. What must a new interdiction do to obtain a strictly larger distance?

1Here is the path that needs to be broken

It uses exactly two arcs: (2,5) and (5,3). As long as both survive, the follower can still travel for 5, whatever happens elsewhere.

2Close a different road: nothing improves

Closing (4,1) is a feasible budget-one interdiction. But it does not touch the blue path. The follower can still use that path at length 5: the interdiction cannot strictly improve the incumbent.

This is what the supervalid inequality removes: decisions that cannot beat the incumbent, even though they may be feasible for the original interdiction model.

3Now hit the selected route

Deleting (2,5) destroys the selected path; the new shortest surviving route shown in blue costs 10.

Deleting (5,3) also breaks the path, and the shortest distance rises to 9. Both are genuine improvements in this example.

5The inequality now makes sense

A decision that strictly improves 5 has to interdict at least one of the two blue arcs. We can therefore demand:

x₂₅ + x₅₃ ≥ 1

It does not say that every feasible interdiction must satisfy this! It says that any candidate capable of beating the current incumbent must satisfy it. That's why the cut is called supervalid.

Don't use it blindly as a globally valid constraint. It deliberately removes feasible but non-improving decisions. Incumbent handling in the solution algorithm must respect this interpretation.

And what changes with multiple requested pairs?

We draw a shortest path for each requested pair. We combine their arcs into one support. If an interdiction avoids that entire support, all selected paths remain usable, so the total shortest-path objective cannot beat the incumbent.

For many requested paths, the SVI is still the same idea: hit at least one arc in the union of selected paths. Never require a hit in every individual path: increasing one requested distance can suffice to improve the total.

The v2 paper also gives a root-subset condition and a cardinality strengthening; both extend this intuition with provable upper bounds.
