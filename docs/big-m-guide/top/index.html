M-top — a safe length bound

Imagine closing a road on a map

You are planning a trip through a small network. The shortest route uses a particular road. Now that road is closed. Your navigation app must find a different route.

In the real network

A closed road simply cannot be used. You must follow a surviving detour, even if it takes longer.

In our mathematical model

For convenience, we keep the road in the graph, but make it more expensive by adding a penalty called M.

How big should that penalty be? Big enough that the fake shortcut cannot beat the real detour. But not absurdly large: oversized penalties make the optimization model harder to solve.

A concrete example from the paper

Look at the arc 2 → 1. Its original length is 3. With interdiction budget 2, closing this arc uses up the entire budget, so no other arc can be closed. The shortest detour is 2 → 5 → 4 → 1, with length 1 + 3 + 3 = 7.

Original road: 3   ·   Surviving detour: 7

We need 3 + M ≥ 7, hence M ≥ 4.

Notice: choosing M = 12 would also prevent the fake shortcut, but it would be needlessly large. Our aim is a small, safe M.

Only now: what do “upper bound” and “lower bound” mean?

When we cannot calculate every possible detour exactly, we use guarantees.

An upper bound = a ceiling

We want a number that is at least as large as the true surviving distance to the end of the closed arc. One guaranteed surviving path gives us such a ceiling: the shortest surviving path cannot be longer than it.

A lower bound = a floor

We want a number that is no larger than the true surviving distance to the beginning of the closed arc. The original shortest-path distance works: removing roads cannot make travel shorter.

Why use a high estimate for one distance and a low estimate for the other? Because we are trying to be conservative about the extra penalty: make the possible detour look longer, and the distance already traveled look shorter. This gives a penalty that cannot be too small.

One sentence to remember: “Upper-bound the distance we must match; lower-bound the distance we have already paid; subtract the original road length.”

Now the formula in the next section has a meaning: M = maximum of 0 and (distance ceiling − distance floor − original arc length). The remaining lessons show how to build better ceilings and floors from the graph.

Before the four rules · the fundamental idea

What do we overestimate? What do we underestimate?

Suppose arc a = (u,v) has been interdicted and we look at shortest paths from a root w. Write dI for distances in the residual graph, after removing the interdicted arcs.

An interdicted arc must not create an artificially short route to its head. The fundamental comparison is:

OVERestimate the distance to the head

Find a safe upper bound U on the residual distance dI(w,v). A certified surviving w→v path gives such an upper bound: the true shortest path cannot be longer than a path that survives.

U ≥ dI(w,v)

UNDERestimate the distance to the tail

Find a safe lower bound L on dI(w,u). In particular, the original shortest-path distance d(w,u) is a lower bound: deleting arcs cannot make a shortest path shorter.

L ≤ dI(w,u)

Required penalty: M(a,w) ≥ dI(w,v) − dI(w,u) − ℓa
Safe construction: M(a,w) = max{0, U − L − ℓa}
Why it is valid: because U is at least the true distance to the head and L is at most the true distance to the tail, U − L − ℓa is an upper bound on the penalty we actually need. We want that upper bound as small as possible while keeping it safe.

A numerical picture

Imagine the residual distance to the head is 13, the residual distance to the tail is 4, and the removed arc's original length is 3. The exact requirement for this interdiction is 13 − 4 − 3 = 6. If we only know U = 15 and L = 3, we safely choose M = 15 − 3 − 3 = 9. It is valid, but 3 units larger than necessary.

This is the central logic behind all four rules: top constructs upper bounds from lengths; flow improves them using surviving paths; rtop subtracts a root-to-tail lower bound; rflow combines both improvements.

For a valid choice across all feasible interdictions I containing a, the upper- and lower-bound guarantees must hold for every such I. The displayed numerical illustration explains the inequality; the lesson pages use the paper's actual graph.

The idea

What this rule is trying to do

The first rule deliberately ignores most of the network's structure. It uses one simple fact: a simple detour contains at most n−1 arcs.

Detailed walkthrough

Derivation, step by step

1

Remove impossible arcs. For a=(2,1), remove (2,1), every arc entering vertex 2, and every arc leaving vertex 1. Those arcs cannot belong to a simple 2→1 detour.

2

Sort the remaining lengths. The four largest eligible lengths are 5, 4, 3, 3. This is a sum of lengths, not a real path.

3

Convert length to penalty. The selected lengths sum to 15. Since ℓ(2,1)=3, the valid top penalty is 15−3=12.

\[M_{\mathrm{top}}(a)=\max\Bigl\{0,\sum_{j=1}^{\min\{n-1,m_a\}}\ell_{e_j(a)}-\ell_a\Bigr\}\]
The figure matters: the 5+4+3+3 selected arcs need not form one contiguous path. This explains why the upper bound can greatly overestimate the actual detour.
